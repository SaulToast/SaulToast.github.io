## Game overview

Ultrawave is a top-down space combat game built in C# on a custom game framework using MonoGame. Players fight through escalating enemy encounters, collect resources and experience, and develop their ship through combat upgrades and hub progression. A run moves from survival into a boss encounter before returning the player to the hub.

I am one of three developers. All three of us collaborate on game design, including enemies, weapons, buildings, and progression. My engineering work turns those designs into enemy behaviors, weapon implementations, and reusable gameplay components.

## Enemy AI

I contributed enemy concepts and implemented enemy behaviors and transition conditions. Each enemy needs movement, attacks, animation, and warning effects to work together, so its intent is readable and the player has a chance to react.

The state-based framework composes those actions into attack sequences. A state can move, fire, stop, change animation, or begin a telegraph. Conditions determine when to transition based on time, distance, energy, health, or animation completion.

### Coordinating an attack sequence

The Elephant approaches, stops to telegraph a dash, charges a defined distance, and then recovers. Splitting that sequence into states lets movement, the visual warning, attack completion, and cooldown participate in the same behavior flow.

<ProcessFlow label="Elephant attack sequence" steps={['Approach', 'Telegraph', 'Charge', 'Recover']} />

The same framework supports different constraints: the Hunter recharges when its energy is depleted, Fury can interrupt its normal behavior with dormancy, and Iron King chooses attacks with their own telegraph and recovery states.

<details>
<summary>Implementation detail: behaviors and conditions</summary>

`AIBehaviorStateMachine` tracks the active state and an optional global override. Each `BState` contains behaviors and conditional transitions. Conditions are evaluated before behaviors run, and a matching condition may choose randomly among several destination states. `BBehavior` supplies enter, update, and exit hooks, allowing actions to be composed without putting an entire enemy's logic into a single update method.

Global transitions can interrupt the current behavior. The override is cleared after transitioning; Fury re-arms its dormancy transition when needed.

</details>

## Weapons

I helped design every weapon with the team and wrote the weapon code. Each weapon controls its firing pattern while sharing input, lifecycle, owner, and spawning interfaces. The objects created by an attack then combine travel, collision, damage, lifetime, and presentation components.

This supports weapons with different engineering requirements:

- **Axiom:** semi-automatic fire with an unlockable staged charge shot.
- **Disperser:** spread fire that can become a single slug, with slug damage incorporating pellet count.
- **Arclance:** a returning projectile and unlockable orbiting secondary projectiles.
- **LightBlade:** a sustained beam with damage ticks and modifiers such as pulse, pull, and turret beams.

### Keeping active attacks in sync

A weapon upgrade can affect an attack that already exists. LightBlade explicitly refreshes the damage of its active beam when damage upgrades apply; certain upgrades also require refreshing turret beams. Updating the weapon's data alone would leave these active attacks using values they had already cached.

<details>
<summary>Implementation detail: the shared weapon contract</summary>

`IWeapon` covers press/release input, updating, service binding, owner synchronization, cleanup, and a bullet-fired event. Optional secondary behavior uses `ISecondaryFireWeapon`. `BaseWeapon` handles shared projectile quantity and scale behavior, creating spread volleys by cloning spawn descriptors and rotating their target directions. Target and muzzle-position interfaces provide aiming information from the owner.

</details>

## Reusable gameplay components

I wrote many of the behaviors, conditions, drops, pickups, and other components used by Ultrawave's gameplay. These run on a shared entity/component framework built around `WeatheredObject`, which combines movement, collision, behavior, and presentation in individual objects.

### Assembling objects with Creatables

I worked with Creatables to assemble and spawn gameplay objects. These factory methods construct a `WeatheredObject` from reusable components: a projectile needs travel, collision, damage, lifetime, and graphics; an enemy adds AI, targeting, health, and drops.

<ProcessFlow label="From gameplay action to a spawned object" steps={['Spawn descriptor', 'Entity service & factory', 'Creatable + components', 'Initialize & register']} />

`SpawnDescriptor` carries an archetype name, transform, persistence information, and an `Extra` dictionary of creation parameters. The factory resolves the archetype to its construction function. The entity service then binds services, initializes the object's family, registers colliders, and raises the spawn event.

### Connecting combat to drops and pickups

My drop and pickup work connects combat outcomes to resources. `DropOnDeathComponent` responds to a health death event. `DropComponent` rolls chances and quantities, then requests drop archetypes through the entity service. `AddResourceWithDelayComponent` gates pickup availability with a timer.

This gives each component a distinct responsibility while letting enemy death, spawning, pickup timing, and progression cooperate through shared systems.

## Upgrades & progression

I collaborated on progression and building design and contributed code to the upgrade system. The game connects two progression layers: hub upgrades and choices made during a run.

- **Hub progression** stores upgrade requirements, costs, claim state, and effects. Upgrade nodes can represent branches, but the current weapon tiers use linear chains.
- **Run progression** combines generic, dash, and equipped-weapon options. Eligibility checks filter choices against progression, weapon identity, stack limits, prerequisites, and incompatible tags before weighted selection.

Capability interfaces such as `IAdjustDamage` let effects work with supported weapon features. This connects upgrade selection to the weapon implementations and their changes in behavior.

## Physics integration & debugging

The physics engine was implemented by my teammate. My contribution was using it from gameplay code, connecting spawned objects and collision behavior, and debugging how those systems interacted.

The early capture below makes that distinction visible: the debug box represents the ship's actual collider, and the resolver prevents it from intersecting another box. The ship artwork and collision geometry are separate parts of the interaction.

<details>
<summary>Shared framework context</summary>

The physics service gathers nearby candidates with a quadtree before detailed collision checks. It includes discrete and continuous paths, layer masks, raycasts, overlap queries, and physical response. Continuous handling is especially relevant to fast projectiles.

`WeatheredObject` supports parent/child hierarchies for attached hitboxes and effects. The entity service queues creation, destruction, and despawning, keeping collider registration aligned with object lifecycle. These are shared framework systems I worked with, rather than a claim that I authored the physics engine or every service.

</details>

## Asset integration

I integrated art assets into the gameplay pipeline, connecting sprite sheets and animation data to runtime objects and effects. My contribution was the technical integration; asset creation and UI implementation were outside my role.

The atlas pipeline reads packed frame metadata—including rotation, trimmed offsets, original size, and pivots—to preserve sprite placement and animation alignment. Category wrappers expose named sprites and animations to gameplay code, while collision geometry is loaded separately from exported hitbox data.
